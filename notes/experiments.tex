\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This note records the rebuilt empirical evaluation. Part~\ref{part:ms} is the
experiments material; Part~\ref{part:app} holds the supporting measurements.
Every model shares one architecture, optimiser, EMA decay, batch size and
training budget, and is scored through one FID implementation; only the
generative process and its sampler differ. The network regresses the drift of
the bridge SDE rather than the endpoint, which is the same objective weighted by
$(1-s)^{-2}$ and is the weight that the generation error depends on; on CIFAR-10
this lowers FID at $50$ network evaluations from $42.1$ to $23.2$ at
$\gamma=0.3162$. The free parameter is $\gamma^2=\sigma^2T$, not $\sigma$ and
$T$ separately.
\end{abstract}

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{What is being compared.} Every row in every table is a base
generative process, sampled directly from the process it defines. This is the
class the framework belongs to and the class in which its properties are stated:
a process with marginals in closed form, a probability-flow ODE and therefore
exact likelihoods by instantaneous change of variables, and an iterative
trajectory into which guidance terms, inverse-problem solvers and conditioning
changes can be inserted at each step. Distilled students occupy the same
few-evaluation regime and reach lower FID there, by compressing a trained
teacher's trajectory into one to four evaluations; they are a stage applied to a
trained process rather than a process, they require that teacher first, and the
same compression applies to the bridge. The comparison here is therefore among
base processes at one shared training budget, and
Section~\ref{sec:claims} scopes the claims to that.

\paragraph{Models and training.} One UNet per dataset, of the architecture used
in the original experiments: $7.28$\,M parameters on MNIST and $52.54$\,M on
CIFAR-10, $64$ base channels, attention at $16\times16$ and $8\times8$, no
dropout and no class conditioning. Every method in every table uses this network
unchanged, so no result below can be attributed to architecture. Training is
AdamW at $2\times10^{-4}$, $500$-step linear warmup then constant, gradient-norm
clipping at $1.0$, batch size $128$, $40\,000$ steps, an exponential moving
average of the weights at decay $0.9999$ with ramp-in, and bfloat16 autocast.
Data is $32\times32$ in $[-1,1]$; MNIST is zero-padded to $32\times32$ and
replicated to three channels. Sampling and evaluation code is shared by every
method. The final checkpoint is evaluated; no checkpoint selection by FID is
performed.

\paragraph{Hardware and training cost.} All runs are on one NVIDIA GB10 (DGX
Spark, $119$\,GB unified memory, compute capability $12.1$). CIFAR-10 training
runs at $2.84$ iterations per second, so $40\,000$ steps take $3.9$ hours per
model; MNIST is faster. No job runs concurrently with another, so reported
throughput is uncontended.

\paragraph{Learning target.} The network regresses the drift of the bridge SDE,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:drift}
  b(\xib_s,s)=\frac{\E[Y\mid\xib_s]-\xib_s}{1-s},
  \qquad\text{against the conditional target}\quad
  \frac{y-\xib_s}{1-s}=(y-x)-\gamma\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{1-s}}\,\varepsilon,
\end{equation}
with $s\sim U[0,s_{\max}]$, $s_{\max}=0.999$. The drift is what every sampler
integrates. Regressing the endpoint $\E[Y\mid\xib_s]$ under a loss uniform in
$s$, as in the submitted version, is the same objective with the terminal region
discounted by $(1-s)^{-2}$; Appendix~\ref{app:weighting} gives the derivation and
the measurement. At $\gamma=0$ equation~\eqref{eq:drift} is the conditional
flow-matching objective exactly, so flow matching is the deterministic member of
the family rather than an external comparator.

\paragraph{FID protocol.} Each entry generates $50\,000$ samples and scores them
against Inception-v3 pool3 statistics computed over the full training split
($60\,000$ images on MNIST, $50\,000$ on CIFAR-10); FID against the test split is
also recorded and moves in lockstep. Generated samples are mapped once from
$[-1,1]$ to 8-bit by a clamped affine map. No per-image rescaling is applied to
either real or generated images and nothing is written to disk in between. MNIST
is converted to three channels by replication and resized to $299\times299$ by
the same bilinear path as CIFAR-10, so the grayscale and colour pipelines differ
only in the input.

\paragraph{Resolution of the measurement.} FID is biased upward at finite sample
count, so the floor was measured rather than assumed, using the comparator a
table entry uses: $n$ held-out real images against the statistics of the full
reference split. On CIFAR-10 this gives $14.60$ at $n=2000$, $5.85$ at $n=5000$
and $3.15$ at $n=10\,000$; the $n=50\,000$ entries in Table~\ref{tab:cifar} sit
near $0.15$. Sampling-seed repeats on MNIST give a standard deviation of
$0.01$--$0.07$, so differences above $0.2$ are resolved there. No seed replicate
was run at $n=50\,000$ on CIFAR-10; differences below $0.3$ FID in
Table~\ref{tab:cifar} are therefore read as ties.

\paragraph{Both DDIM baselines degrade past one hundred evaluations}, reaching
$16.30$ and $13.26$ at $1000$. Clipping $\beta$ at $0.999$ gives the cosine
schedule a terminal signal-to-noise ratio of $\bar\alpha_T=2.4\times10^{-9}$, at

the deterministic sampler: the ancestral samplers are flat over the same range,
and two independent DDIM timestep grids reproduce it. The prescribed remedy is
$v$-prediction, which is not applied here. It is reported because a baseline
that degrades inflates the margin over it.

\section{Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\paragraph{$\sigma$ and $T$ are not separately identifiable.} Writing $t=sT$,
the bridge marginal is $\xib_s=(1-s)x+sy+\gamma\sqrt{s(1-s)}\,\varepsilon$ with
$\gamma^2=\sigma^2T$. Training and sampling depend on $\sigma$ and $T$ only
through $\gamma$. Section~3 of the submitted manuscript reports $T=0.1$ after
preliminary tuning and Section~2.4 defers $\sigma$ to future work; these are one
parameter, and the published setting is $\gamma=0.3162$.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to $\gamma$.} Figure~\ref{fig:gamma} retrains at five
values of $\gamma$ on MNIST, holding everything else fixed. The deterministic
sampler's penalty grows with $\gamma$ and vanishes at $\gamma=0.1$, where the
two samplers become indistinguishable; the stochastic samplers are comparatively
insensitive. On CIFAR-10 under the corrected objective, $\gamma=0.05$ and
$\gamma=0.3162$ reach $23.34$ and $23.23$ at $50$ evaluations, a difference
within the resolution of that measurement.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the sampler.} Three samplers were run at every step
count: the exact Brownian-bridge posterior, Euler--Maruyama, and the
deterministic Euler map used in the submitted implementation. The first two
agree to within measurement resolution at $\gamma=0.05$ and separate by $5.3$
FID at $\gamma=0.3162$ on CIFAR-10 at $50$ evaluations, with Euler--Maruyama
ahead. The deterministic map converges to a different law;
Appendix~\ref{app:sampler} quantifies the bias on a problem with a closed-form
solution.

\section{Summary of empirical claims}
\label{sec:claims}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item On CIFAR-10 at matched architecture, budget and evaluation code, the
bridge reaches lower FID than improved denoising diffusion below $20$ network
evaluations ($19.21$ against $25.60$ at $10$) and higher FID above it ($9.83$
against $6.04$ at $50$).
\item The bridge and conditional flow matching are level across the range
tested, the largest difference being $1.24$ FID.
\item Quality improves monotonically to $200$ evaluations and rises $3.9\%$ by
$1000$. The same rise appears in improved denoising diffusion at $5.9\%$ and is
absent from the deterministic flow-matching sampler.
\item Cost at equal quality favours the bridge by a factor of $1.2$ in network
evaluations at FID $20$ and favours improved denoising by a factor of $2$ at
FID $10$.
\end{enumerate}

All four are statements about base generative processes sampled directly, at one
shared training budget. They exclude distilled students, which compress a trained
teacher's trajectory rather than defining a process, and which the same
compression would apply to here.

Within that scope the measurements support a claim of a favourable trade-off
between sampling steps and generation quality in the few-evaluation regime. They
do not support claims of best absolute FID, of high-speed generation in general,
or of an order-of-magnitude efficiency advantage.

\section{The training objective is unweighted in bridge time}
\label{app:weighting}

\paragraph{Why the weight matters in practice.} The network's output does not
reach the sample directly. Every sampler advances the state by a multiple of the
drift $b=(\E[Y\mid\xib_s]-\xib_s)/(1-s)$, so an endpoint error $e$ at time $s$
enters the sample at size $e/(1-s)$: the same accuracy is worth $(1-s)^{-2}$
times as much near the data end as at the start, a factor of $100$ at $s=0.9$
and $40\,000$ at $s=0.995$. A Girsanov comparison of the two diffusions, which
share the coefficient $\gamma$, bounds the generated law's error by
$\int_0^1\E\|e\|^2(1-s)^{-2}\,\d s$, while an endpoint loss uniform in $s$
minimises $\int_0^1\E\|e\|^2\,\d s$. The two differ in the weight alone, and both
are minimised at $e\equiv0$, so the submitted code implements the manuscript
correctly and the issue is not one of consistency.

It is an issue of where a finite network puts the error it cannot avoid. Under
$w\equiv1$ the region $s>0.98$ contributes $2\%$ of the loss; under
$w=(1-s)^{-2}$ it contributes nearly all of it. The unweighted loss is
additionally dominated by error no capacity can remove: at $s=0.05$ the state is
nearly independent of $Y$, the best estimate is the dataset mean, and the squared
error is $0.208$ against the $0.0002$ available at $s=0.995$.

\paragraph{Both comparators already carry such a weight.} For DDPM, noise
prediction gives $\|\hat\epsilon-\epsilon\|^2=\mathrm{SNR}(t)\|\hat y-y\|^2$,
endpoint regression tilted towards the data end. For flow matching the linear
path gives $v=(y-z_s)/(1-s)$ identically, so
$\E\|\hat v-v\|^2=\E[\|f_\theta-y\|^2/(1-s)^2]$: the weight the bound calls for,
obtained by a change of variable rather than chosen.

\paragraph{Measurement.} Both networks were trained identically and then
evaluated as endpoint estimators on a common path --- the $\gamma=0$ interpolant,
so neither sees its own training noise --- on $2048$ triples $(x,y,s)$, with the
flow network's output converted by $\hat y=z_s+(1-s)\hat v$.

\paragraph{Effect on FID, and why MNIST does not show it.} At $\gamma=0.3162$ on
CIFAR-10 the correction moves the $50$-evaluation figure from $42.14$ to
$23.23$. The asymmetry in Table~\ref{tab:weighting} is also present on MNIST and
slightly larger by the endpoint ratio, $12.4\times$ at $s=0.995$ against
CIFAR-10's $10.3\times$, yet on MNIST the uncorrected bridge still leads its
baselines. Near-binary digits settle their layout well before $s=0.8$ and the
terminal region carries little that the Inception features respond to;
CIFAR-10 texture is written in that region.

\paragraph{The cost of stochasticity.} For $\gamma>0$ the target
\eqref{eq:drift} carries conditional variance $\gamma^2 s/(1-s)$, whose integral
diverges logarithmically, which is why $s_{\max}<1$. The effect is visible in
training --- the $\gamma=0.3162$ drift loss settles at $0.330$ against
$\gamma=0.05$'s $0.190$ --- and does not reach FID at the noise levels tested
($23.23$ against $23.34$ at $50$ evaluations). Whether it does at larger $\gamma$
is not established.

\section{The deterministic sampler in one dimension}
\label{app:sampler}

The submitted implementation constructs its sampler with
\texttt{deterministic=True}, deleting the diffusion term from
$\d\xib_s=b\,\d s+\gamma\,\d B_s$ and leaving
$\xib_u=\xib_s+\frac{\hat y-\xib_s}{1-s}(u-s)$. Algorithm~2 of the manuscript
includes the diffusion term. A deterministic sampler preserving the marginals
must follow the probability-flow ODE, whose drift carries the additional term
$-\tfrac{\gamma^2}{2}\nabla\log p_s$; without it the scheme matches neither the
SDE nor its flow ODE.

On a one-dimensional problem where $\E[Y\mid\xi_s]$ is available in closed form
($\Phi=\N(0,1)$, $\Psi$ a Gaussian mixture), so all residual error is the
sampler's, the exact posterior and Euler--Maruyama schemes both reach the
Monte-Carlo floor in $1$-Wasserstein distance at $2000$ steps ($0.0096$ and
$0.0095$ against a floor of $0.0091$), while the deterministic map stalls at
$0.0625$, $6.9\times$ the floor, with a terminal variance deficit ($1.726$
against $1.758$). The residual scales with $\gamma$ as the omitted score term
predicts: $1.2\times$ the floor at $\gamma=0.032$, $6.9\times$ at $0.316$,
$41.9\times$ at $1.0$, and $114\times$ at $\gamma=2$, where the terminal standard
deviation falls from $1.758$ to $0.775$.

\section{Numerical check of Proposition 2.13}
\label{app:theory}

This is the one measurement in this document that bears on the theory section,
and it is included because Proposition~2.13 and Remark~2.14 are stated in the
submitted manuscript without numerical support. Proposition~2.13 states that
$\mathrm{Var}[Y\mid\xib_s]$ is a supermartingale in the Loewner order and
Remark~2.14 that it vanishes at the terminal time. The training objective
already estimates the quantity: at bridge time $s$,
$\E\|y-f_\theta(\xib_s,s)\|^2\to\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Var}[Y\mid\xib_s]/n$ once
$f_\theta$ attains the $\mathcal{L}^2$ projection, so a trained network measures
the supermartingale without additional computation.

Two endpoints are predicted exactly. At $s\to1$ the state pins down $y$ and the
value must vanish: measured $9\times10^{-5}$. At $s=0$ the state is independent
of $y$ under the coupling used, the best predictor is $\E[Y]$, and the value must
equal the mean per-pixel variance of the data: theory $0.21201$, measured
$0.21346$, a ratio of $1.007$. The profile decreases across all $21$ sampled
times (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}). A visual form of the same statement: at one
network evaluation the sampler returns $\E[Y]$ and every generated sample is the
mean digit.

\section{FID implementation and measured floors}
\label{app:fid}

Each entry generates $n$ samples and scores them against Inception-v3 pool3
statistics over the full reference split. Generated samples are mapped once from
$[-1,1]$ to 8-bit by a clamped affine map; no per-image rescaling is applied to
real or generated images. MNIST is zero-padded to $32\times32$, replicated to
three channels, and resized to $299\times299$ by the same bilinear path as
CIFAR-10. The matrix square root is computed by eigendecomposition of the
symmetrised product; the implementation returns exactly $0$ on identical inputs
and $2048.0000$ on a unit analytic shift.

The floor uses the comparator a table entry uses: $n$ held-out real images
against the full reference split, not $n$ against $n$. The two differ by a factor
of $1.7$ on CIFAR-10 at $n=2000$ ($14.60$ against $25.00$).
